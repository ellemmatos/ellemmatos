My career does not look like a straight line.

I started with a technical degree in electronics. Then came an electronics internship, IT support, service as a Specialist Sergeant in the Brazilian Army, and a degree in Information Management. After that: SharePoint and .NET development, consulting in Recife and then Lisbon, delivery facilitation, and today project management for AI-enabled engineering teams.

For a long time, a path like this can feel like something to explain away. It is the opposite. **In a market that changes every few months, a zigzag path is proof that you can learn something new and deliver with it.** This article is for anyone with a path like that, and especially for women thinking about a move into a technical or leadership role.

## What most people get wrong

The common reaction to a non-linear path is to hide it or to apologise for it.

You see it on CVs and LinkedIn profiles. Old roles are cut. Job titles are listed with no link between them. In interviews, people start with "I know my background is a bit unusual, but…".

The problem is not the path. The problem is that **the reader has to find the connection alone**, and most readers will not try. A list of unrelated titles looks like drift. The same list with a clear thread looks like range.

## What to do instead

You need three things: the thread, the story and the proof.

### 1. Find the thread

Write down every step of your path. For each one, answer two questions:

- What problem did I solve there?
- What skill did I carry into the next step?

Here is how my own path looks with that exercise:

| Step | What it gave me |
| --- | --- |
| Electronics degree and internship | Systematic troubleshooting: find the root cause, not the symptom |
| IT support | The user's point of view: what people actually struggle with |
| Army, leading a 3-person maintenance team | Leading a small team with clear procedures and responsibility |
| SharePoint and .NET development | How software is really built, tested and shipped |
| Delivery facilitation, early generative AI use | Using AI for documentation, requirements and test planning |
| Project management | Delivery across remote teams, with AI from planning to code |

Read the right column from top to bottom. That is the thread: **solving problems for people, with technology, and bringing a team along.** Your thread will be different. The exercise is the same.

### 2. Turn the thread into one sentence

Recruiters and hiring managers read fast. Give them one sentence that connects the past to the role you want next.

A simple template:

```text
I started in <first field>, moved through <2-3 key steps>,
and that mix is why I can <the value you bring> for <the people you help>.
```

For example: "I started in electronics, moved through the Army and software development, and that mix is why I can lead remote engineering teams that work with AI from planning to code."

Use this sentence at the top of your LinkedIn "About" section, at the start of interviews and in your CV summary.

### 3. Back it with proof

A story without evidence sounds like spin. Add one or two concrete results to each important step. Numbers work best.

My own examples: introducing Scrum from scratch in 7 technical teams and scaling the model to more than 22 teams, and coaching around 15 people from their first AI prompt to working with AI on their own.

If you have no numbers, use scope: team size, number of countries, a system you documented, a process you changed.

### 4. Let AI do the first draft

Mapping a long career to a new job is tedious. This is a good job for an AI assistant, as long as you review every line. Remove personal data you would not share with any tool, and follow your company's rules.

```text
Here is my career history (role, years, main tasks):
<paste your list>

Here is a job description I am interested in:
<paste the job description>

1. For each step of my history, list the skills that match the job.
2. Suggest one sentence that connects my path to this role.
3. List the gaps I should be honest about, and one way to close each.
Do not invent results or numbers. Ask me if something is missing.
```

The last line matters. **Never let the tool add results you did not achieve.** Your story is only strong if it is true.

### 5. Say the move out loud

A path only becomes a direction when you choose the next step. Tell your manager, your mentor or your network which role you want next and why your path prepares you for it. People cannot support a move they do not know about.

## A checklist you can copy

Use this before you update your CV or profile, or before your next interview:

- [ ] Every step of my path is listed, none hidden
- [ ] For each step, I can name one problem I solved and one skill I carried forward
- [ ] I have one sentence that links my past to the role I want
- [ ] I have at least two concrete results, with numbers or scope
- [ ] I do not apologise for my path anywhere
- [ ] I have told at least one person which role I want next

## The takeaway

A zigzag path is not something to defend. It is something to explain well. Find the thread, tell it in one sentence, and back it with proof.
